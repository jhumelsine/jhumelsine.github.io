# Introduction
My previous [Financial Literacy](https://jhumelsine.github.io/2026/11/13/financial-literacy.html) blog introduced elements of a Financial Toolbox that help you prepare for retirement.
There was a lot information that could be a bit overwhelming.
I didn't describe how to use the elements of that toolbox.

This blog entry lays out a personal and household financial plan with retirement as one of the goals.
It will consist of the following financial principles:
* Define goals
* Develop a cash flow margin
* Save and invest consistently
* Leverage compound interest
* Avoid bad debt and leverage good debt judiciously
* Automate

This blog may require some self-examination and introspection.
This blog may require you to make some tough choices.

I do not endorse _get rich quick_ schemes.
This is a _slow and steady wins the race_ plan.
This is a plan that's based upon [your earning ability](https://jhumelsine.github.io/2026/11/13/financial-literacy.html#you) and  leveraging the power of [compound interest](https://jhumelsine.github.io/2026/11/13/financial-literacy.html#time) over the years.

# Goals
Retirement is the primary financial goal of this blog, but it's not the only one for most households.

Other financial goals include:
* Purchasing a Home
* Saving for college for children
* Paying off student loans
* Purchasing the next car
* Taking vacations
* Etc.

Achieving one's goals is not an either/or situation or a sequential endeavor where only one goal is addressed at a time.
A household can work toward multiple goals simultaneously.
Some goals may have higher priority than others.
There may be times, such as during a period of unemployment, when funding lower priority goals needs to be suspended temporarily.

# Live Beneath Your Means
**_Annual income twenty pounds, annual expenditure nineteen nineteen and six, result happiness. Annual income twenty pounds, annual expenditure twenty pounds ought and six, result misery._** — Wilkins Micawber in _David Copperfield_ by Charles Dickens

Living beneath your means is a key financial principle in your plan.
When you live at your means, you’re living paycheck to paycheck.
It's hand to mouth.
You’ll get by, but you’ll never get ahead, and you’ll be living on the razor’s edge.
One miscalculation or bit of bad luck, and you'll find yourself behind the financial eight ball.
If you live beyond your means, then you’re constantly digging yourself into a debt hole that gets deeper over time.
 
Living beneath your means produces the additional money that makes your financial goals a possibility.
As described by Dickens above, it's your income minus your expenses, which is also known as your margin.
Living beneath your means is critical in funding your financial goals.
The more margin you have, the more flexibility you have to achieve your goals and by what degree.

Ideally, your income will naturally be greater than your expenses.
That is, your lifestyle expenses don't exceed your income, and you have margin without having to make any adjustments.

When don't have sufficient margin, you have three options:
* [Spend less](#spend-less)
* [Earn more](#earn-more)
* [Sell something](#sell-something)

## Spend Less
Track your expenses.
For a month or more, record every purchase you make, even that stick of gum at the checkout counter. Don’t record an ATM withdrawal as a purchase, unless there’s an ATM fee, but do record what you buy with that money.
 
Group like purchases into similar categories, such as housing, insurance, groceries, utilities, entertainment, dining out, etc.
 
Next, analyze your spending categories.
Which ones are **needs** and which ones are **wants**?
Needs are expenses that must be addressed, such as housing, food, etc.
Wants are expenses that tend to be more flexible, such as entertainment.

Create a spending plan based upon your needs and wants.
You may need to make some tough decisions about how you spend your money.
There's generally not a lot of flexibility in needs, but there's usually some flexibility in wants in a spending plan.
A spending plan forces you to make a conscious effort to direct your income where you choose.

I prefer the term _spending plan_ rather than _budget_, even if they are similar.
The former focuses upon your spending priorities.
The latter focuses upon restrictions.

A spending plan might include needs and wants, such as:
* Taxes
* Housing
* Utilities
* Debt
* Food and other necessities
* Fun/Entertainment
* Goals

Spending less is the quickest action to implement, but one of the toughest to maintain. Cutting spending is like going on a diet: it takes willpower, because you may need to change old spending habits.

## Earn More
Additional earnings can come from finding a better paying job, working overtime, taking a second job, getting more training or education, or starting your own business.
 
However, care is needed. Extra money might encourage extra spending. If you're not careful, you could end up with even more financial troubles. Many high-income American families live paycheck to paycheck.

## Sell Something
Turn a possession into cash. You might have a small amount to sell, through a yard sale or eBay.
Or, you might need to sell a significant item, such as a boat, car or property.
Boats, cars and property often include ongoing expenses, such as maintenance and insurance.
These disappears when you sell the item, so selling something that also has an ongoing expense falls into the [spend less](#spend-less) category too.
 
Clean up your home. Remove the clutter. Sell, donate or throw out the items you don’t use anymore.
Most of us don’t think of the cost of the items in our homes.
They can cost us in: money, space, time and sanity.
You have to spend time and money to clean, maintain and possibly insure these items.
How many people rent storage space to store things they never use?
How many people buy houses larger than needed because of their material possessions?

# The Plan
Once you have a set of preliminary goals and your positive cash flow margin, decide how to use the margin to reach your goals.

Each plan will be different depending upon goals and margin.
The plan I've listed below is one suggestion.

I describe the plan sequentially, but it's not necessarily meant to be executed sequentially.
Some parts should be done in parallel, and I'll highlight any that I feel are important.
The degree to parallel achievement will be based upon your margin.
Some of these goals have a long time horizon.
For example, investing for retirement is a goal that's sustained throughout one's entire career.

## Establish an Emergency Fund
Emergencies happen unexpectedly at the worst time.
Cars need repairs.
Appliances need to be replaced.
Layoffs happen.
None of these are part of your spending plan.

Create an emergency fund to help smooth over these financial bumps.

An emergency fund is a [savings account](https://jhumelsine.github.io/2026/11/13/financial-literacy.html#savings-account) or [money market account](https://jhumelsine.github.io/2026/11/13/financial-literacy.html#money-market-account) dedicated exclusively for emergencies.
It's used for unexpected expenses and periods of unemployment.
It is not used for regular expenses, gifts, vacations, a wedding or other expected expense.

Opinions on how much to reserve in an emergency fund vary.
Three to six months of living expense is the minimum goal, but some recommend as high as 12 months of living expenses.

**NOTE:** An emergency fund should cover emergency living expenses during a period of unemployment, not your usual spending plan expenses while employed.
Unemployment is a period of zero income.
Your previous spending plan no longer applies.
Reduce or suspend the needs and wants in your spending plan, mostly your wants.
Hopefully the period of unemployment won't last for long.
And when you do get your next job, your salary will be different than your previous job.
You'll need to adjust your spending plan accordingly as well.

Dedicate your full margin to your emergency fund until it reaches about one month of emergency living expenses.
Then you can reduce your emergency fund contributions to half of their previous level and use the other half of your margin for other goals.

Continue with half margin funding of your emergency fund until you reach your goal of three, six or 12 months of living expenses depending upon your level of comfort.

An emergency fund is like a spare tire.
You don't want to have to use it, but when you need to use it, you're grateful that it's there.
Replacing a flat tire with the spare only provides you enough mobility to repair or replace your flat tire.
In the interim, you're driving without another spare, so you want to keep that time without a spare to a minimum.

Once your emergency fund is fully funded, consider funding it at a reduced rate so that when you do need to use it, you don't have to change your plan drastically to replenish it.
It's like ensuring that you'll always have a spare tire when you need it.

Since my audience is mostly software engineers, I'll include a software analogy.
An emergency fund is similar to the [Token Bucket Algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Token_bucket).
Your income is similar to the token rate.
Your emergency fund is similar to the token bucket reserve.
An emergency fund provides financial resources when needed.
The token bucket provides access to the network when needed.

## Eliminate Consumer Debt
[Compound interst](https://jhumelsine.github.io/2026/11/13/financial-literacy.html#time) can work for you, or it can work against you.
It works against you when you take on debt.

Consumer debt is unsecured debt.
That is, there's no collateral.
As a result, consumer debt tends to charge high interest rates, since the lenders have limited recourse if you default.
Credit card interest rate can often be 18% or higher because of the additional risk they take for the unsecured debt.
That means that you'll need to pay $180 annually for each $1,000 of debt just to service the debt.

Eliminating consumer debt is one of the best investments you can make.
Paying down an 18% credit card debt is the equivalent of investing in a completely safe investment with an 18% rate of return.
A safe 18% investment does not exist.

Making minimum payments on debt should have already been in the **needs** of your spending plan.
By eliminating consumer debt, the amount that had been dedicated to servicing your debt can become part of your margin to help achieve other goals.
That $180 you were paying solely to service a $1,000 of debt is added to your margin once the $1,000 has been paid off.

Once you've reached a minimum [emergency fund](#establish-an-emergency-fund), focus upon consumer debt.
Consider all of your consumer debt, which will mostly be credit cards with an outstanding balance.
Make the minimum payment for all cards, which should be part of your spending plan.
Then chose and implement a strategy to pay off the debt on your cards one by one.

There are two strategies for this:
* [Debt Snowball Method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt_snowball_method) - Use your available margin to pay off the card with the smallest balance first. Once that debt is eliminated, its previous minimum payment adds to your margin. Use your adjusted margin to pay off the next smallest balance. Continue until all balances are paid off.
* [Debt Avalanche Method](https://www.nerdwallet.com/finance/learn/what-is-a-debt-avalanche) - Use this method to pay off the card with the highest rate first. Then once it's paid off, focus upon the card with the next highest rate. Repeat until all cards are paid off.

Avalanche is the most efficient, since highest rates are paid down first, but Snowball provides more motivation, since progress is more visible as individually cards are retired more quickly.
Ideally, the two methods align in that the debt with the smallest balance is also at the highest rate.

I'm not advocating avoiding credit cards.
I use my credit card all the time.
It's convenient.
It provides a level of protection.
And I earn rewards.

However, I pay my credit card debt in full each month.
Do the same.
Once consumer debt is eliminated, do not go back into credit card debt.
Your spending plan and emergency fund should keep you out of credit card debt, but if it cannot, then you may need to remove credit cards from regular use until you have sufficient margin to pay off your credit cards each month.
