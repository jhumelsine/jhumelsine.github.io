# Live Beneath Your Means
_Annual income twenty pounds, annual expenditure nineteen nineteen and six, result happiness. Annual income twenty pounds, annual expenditure twenty pounds ought and six, result misery._ — Wilkins Micawber in _David Copperfield_ by Charles Dickens

Living beneath your means is the key financial principle.
When you live at your means, you’re living paycheck to paycheck, or hand to mouth.
You’ll get by, but you’ll never get ahead.
And you’ll be living on the razor’s edge.
If you live beyond your means, then you’re digging yourself into a debt hole that gets deeper over time.
 
Living beneath your means produces the additional money that makes your financial goals a possibility.
As described by Dickens above, it's your income minus your expenses, which is also known as your margin.
Living beneath your means is critical in fund your goals.
The more margin you have, the goals you can address simultaneously and do what degree.

Ideally, your income is naturally greater than your expenses.
When don't have sufficient margin, you have three options:
* [Spend less](#spend-less)
* Earn more
* Sell something

## Spend Less
<img src="https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/44/STARBUCKS_COFFEE_Tottori.jpg" alt="Starbucks Drive Thru" title="Image Source: https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:STARBUCKS_COFFEE_Tottori.jpg" width = "50%" align="right" style="padding: 20px;">

For a month, record every purchase you make, even that stick of gum at the checkout counter. Don’t record an ATM withdrawal as a purchase, unless there’s an ATM fee, but do record what you buy with that money.
 
After the period is over, group like purchases into similar categories, such as dining out, utilities, entertainment, etc.
 
Next, analyze your spending to determine if it meets your goals. Make adjustments based upon this analysis and this will become the basis for your spending plan.

A spending plan might look like this:
* Taxes - The government often takes its cut first. We live off our net income, not our gross income
* Goals - Saving for retirement, a house, or college
* Bills - We pay the mortgage, insurance and the utilities
* Food and other necessities
* Fun / entertainment

I prefer the term _spending plan_ rather than _budget_, even if they are similar.
The former focuses upon your spending priorities.
The latter focuses upon restrictions.

A spending plan forces you to make a conscious effort to direct your income where you choose for it to go.

Spending less is the quickest action to implement, but one of the toughest to maintain. Cutting spending is like going on a diet: it takes willpower, because you need to change old habits.

People who overspend may suffer from thoughtless spending.
Author [David Bach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bach_(author)) coined the term [Latte Factor](https://www.becomingminimalist.com/latte-factor/) about 25 years ago.
Bach coined the term based upon a conversation he had with a woman at a seminar.
She complained that she didn't have any margin to fund her retirement.
In his brief interaction with her, she revealed that she stopped for coffee before work almost every morning on the way to work and usually picked up a muffin too. A coworker was with her and chimed in that she purchased another one during their mid-morning coffee break. Bach did a little back-of-the-envelop math and quickly showed her drinking habit consuming her potential margin.

Latte Factor has become a bit cliché, and many complain that he's blaming the victim, but let's do a little math.

There's almost always a line in the Starbucks Drive Thru near my house. That doesn't include the customers who are inside.
Starbucks advertises their lattes from about $5 to $7 depending upon size and preparation. Let's set an average of $6 per latte.

Let's assume that someone treats themselves three times per week 50 weeks per year. That's __$6 * 3 * 50__ or __$900__ annually.
Remember that [Anna and Billy](https://jhumelsine.github.io/2026/11/13/financial-literacy.html#a-comparison-example) contributed $1,000 annually into their retirement plans in sequence throughout their careers. Let's assume they had a third sibling who contributed $1,000 annually throughout their entire career. That sibling would have $790,795 at retirement.

Thoughtless spending isn't just coffee.
It can be any regular relatively small purchase that adds up over time, such as smoking, lottery tickets, multiple streaming service subscriptions, the gym membership that's never used, etc.

Buying lunch every day is a spending plan killer.
A packed lunch from home will cost less than what's available at the office or going out for lunch.

This doesn't mean that you never indulge yourself.
Just don't do it as frequently, especially if you have a tight spending plan.

### Spouse
If you’re in a committed relationship, you and your partner need to work as a team. This doesn’t mean that each partner is expected to produce 50% of the household income no pay 50% of the household expenses. Your household may live off of one income. One of you may have a head for numbers and the other not quite so.
 
Regardless, you need to be equally yoked in your responsibility toward the management of the money in your household. Don’t pull against each other financially. This isn’t a case of my money or your money. It’s not even our money. It’s our family's money.
 
There are many ways for couples to manage their household finances as a team.
 
Schedule regular “money dates” to discuss household finances and goals. Your goals should be consistent regardless of how each person contributes toward those goals. At the beginning these money dates should be weekly, as the household finances progress, they can be more sporadic.
 
In these meetings, clarify goals and monitor your progress in obtaining them. As goals, or even sub goals are met, celebrate the progress. Go out on a real date. Take a walk on the boardwalk. Just make sure you acknowledge your progress.
 
As for day to day finances, for some families, a common account works. For others separate accounts work. For some there’s a common account for major household purchases and smaller separate accounts for personal purchases.
 
There is no one perfect way to do this. Every family may be different. What’s important is that everyone in the family is sharing the same vision and each agrees with the approach taken.

## Seek a Professional
Michael Jordan was arguably the best basketball player in a generation. Yet even when he was the best there was, he also had coaches and trainers. Why? Because even the best can be made better, through careful management and keeping Michael focused.
 
Investors with financial planners tend to have greater assets than those who don’t. This isn’t so much from better returns, insider deals or fabulous management as it is from more focused investing. People with financial planners tend to invest more, be more focused, and stay with the plan.
 
There is too much detail in the personal financial management world for an amateur to understand it all. One would be wise to seek professional help. A professional will manage most of the paperwork. Additionally, the professional planner understands the math and know how to use [financial calculators](https://www.google.com/search?q=financial+calculators&sec_src=docs) in order to quantify your goals to know how large they need to be (e.g., how big does my nest egg need to be for a comfortable retirement?) and how much needs to be allocated toward a goal on a regular basis to meet that goal on schedule.
 
The two most important characteristics of a financial planner are:
* You need to trust your planner.
* Your planner has to share your same core values.
 
Ask friends for references. Interview several planners. Ask for their investment philosophy. Ask how they are paid, which varies depending upon the type of planner. Ask about their training. Ask for references from their clients. Ask how they are compensated.

# Tale as Old as Time
Though financial instruments have emerged and have created new investment options, the foundations of personal and household finance go back for millennia.

[_The Richest Man in Babylon_](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Richest_Man_in_Babylon), first published in 1926, describes the path to wealth as revealed in a set of four thousand year old proverbs from the fictional character, Arkad, an ancient scribe, who became the richest man in Babylon.

It includes sage advice such as:
* _For every ten coins thou placest within thy purse take out for use but nine. Thy purse will start to fatten at once and its increasing weight will feel good in thy hand and bring satisfaction to thy soul._
* _The earnings it will make shall build your fortunes ... Learn to make your treasure work for you. Make it your slave. Make its children and its children's children work for you._

This advice was author [George Samuel Clason's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Samuel_Clason) stylized version of [Live Beneath Your Means](#you) and the [Power of Compound Interest](#time).

Clason imagined wealth management in the ancient world, but we don't need to.
Many [religious texts](https://www.google.com/search?q=List+the+personal+and+household+financial+advice+contained+with+the+religious+texts+of+the+world) contain passages relating to personal and household financial management.

Most modern financial principles can trace their origins back to these texts.
This summary highlights a few financial principals from those texts:
* **Debt Avoidance & Fair Lending:** Minimize borrowing to preserve personal freedom, avoid predatory high-interest lending, and prefer risk-sharing partnerships over pure debt.
* **Ethical Wealth Creation:** Earn a living honestly without exploiting workers, use accurate trade measures, pay laborers immediately, and reject fast, deceptive wealth schemes.
* **Systematic Income Allocation:** Live below your means and structurally divide income into fixed buckets for daily expenses, business reinvestment, and emergency reserves.
* **Structured Generosity:** Automate charitable giving using fixed percentages (typically 2.5% to 20%) to support the vulnerable while preserving their dignity.
* **Stewardship Mindset:** Prioritize contentment over endless accumulation, viewing money as a temporary resource to be managed responsibly for family and social good.

# Retirement Pillars
Retirement is supported by several pillars, often called the [_Retirement Stool_](https://www.investopedia.com/ask/answers/09/three-legged-stool-retirement.asp) consistenting of:
* Employer Pension
* Government Pension
* Personal Saving and Investing

The Retirement Stool is an antiquated model that doesn't apply to most current workers.
Employer Pensions are mostly a thing of the past, except for certain sectors, such as public service jobs.
Government Pensions, such as [Social Security](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_Security_(United_States)) in the United States, are at risk of insolvency due to shifting demographics.
That is the population is getting older, and we don't have as many younger employers to subsidize pensions for retired seniors.

The only pillar in control for most people is Personal Saving and Investing.
This will be the only aspect of retirement plan that I'll focus on in the rest of the blog.

# Fear and Greed
The two primary emotions with money are **fear** and **greed**.
They feel different, yet when I try to define them with respect to money, I keep coming back to the same definition for each:
* **Fear** - _The emotion one has when they don't have enough money._
* **Greed** - _The emotion one has when they don't have enough money._

The distinction is context.
Those with fear are concerned that they will never have ehough.
Those with greed are concerned that they won't have be able to keep what they have.

Fear paralyzes. Greed hoards.

Saving and investing addresses fear.
Giving and generosity addresses greed.

I'll mostly focus upon saving and investing, but I want to address giving and generosity first.
Consider giving as part of your financial practice.
It doesn't matter what you support.
Be generous to your house of worship, university, medical research, the arts, and so on.
Any amount will do.

Don't give because of what you will do for the charity.
Give because of what being altruistic will do for you.

# Finances
_The best time to plant a tree was twenty years ago. The second best time is now._ — Chinese Proverb

I think there's a fourth leg to the stool that's rarely mentioned: Windfalls.

Windfalls come in several forms:
* Lottery - Yes, there are big winners, but they are few and far between. Please don't play the lottery regularly. I favor [Fran Lebowitz](https://www.goodreads.com/quotes/85215-i-figure-you-have-the-same-chance-of-winning-the) philosophy with respect to the lottery: _I figure you have the same chance of winning the lottery whether you play or not._ If you want to buy a ticket when the jackpot grows to incredible levels, then have a little fun, but treat it only as entertainment while dreaming about the possibilities for a few hours, because that's all you'll receive.
* Gambling - I lump several categories into this: Online Gambling, Prediction Markets, Day Trading, Futures/Options, Cryptocurrencies, etc. There may be some upside for these, but I suspect that most participants lose money. Much like the lottery, if it's too alluring to ignore, treat it as entertainment, set aside a fixed budget of money to play with and have fun. Good luck to you.
* Stock Options - Yes, there are big winners here too, but don't count on it. I joined a pre-IPO start up in 2000. My options, when vested, had a conservative value of $8M-US when compared with our public competition. Then the dot-com bubble burst. Our post-IPO start up imploded a few years later. My vested options netted a total profit of about $21.22-US. I tried not to blow it all on a yacht.
* Inheritance - Yes, there are those who inherit substantial sums of money. Trillions of US dollars will be transferred from the older generations to younger generations through the [Great Wealth Transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wealth_Transfer) in the next two decades. However, inheritance is not equally distributed nor can you determine when you receive it. Your best bet for a significant inheritance is to choose your parents wisely.
