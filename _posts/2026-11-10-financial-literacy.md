# Introduction
Many people enter adulthood without a solid foundation in personal finance, relying instead on trial and error, peers, books, or their parents to figure things out.

My parents were financially conservative.
The financial instruments I knew about were generally limited to:
* [Bank Savings Accounts](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/s/savingsaccount.asp)
* [Bank Certificates of Deposit](https://www.investopedia.com/terms/c/certificateofdeposit.asp)
* [United States Savings Bonds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Savings_Bonds)

Their financially conservative nature also applied to their spending.
They didn't make major purchases until they had the money to pay for them.

Their financial discipline worked for them, and it left an impression on me.
This was the only approach to spending and saving that I knew while growing up.
I was equally conservative in my spending and investing when I first entered the workforce.

A few years into my career, I partnered with a financial planner, and my financial education began. My financial planner introduced me to a much broader range of financial instruments and investment opportunities than I knew growing up. I learned that there were different ways to approach saving and investing, depending on my circumstances and financial goals.

The financial discipline I learned from my parents provided a solid foundation. Working with my financial planner expanded my understanding of personal finance and the options available to me. Together, these experiences shaped the approach to personal finance that has served me throughout my life.

Over the years, I've learned quite a bit about personal finance. I'd like to share some of what I've learned, beginning with a look at the basic concepts and instruments that make up a financial toolkit.

# Financial Toolkit
Income and wealth are related, but not the same.
Sometimes we hear stories of high-paid athletes struggling with their personal finances and eventually filing for bankruptcy.
In contrast, we sometimes hear stories of those who never earned stellar salaries, yet accumulated millions in wealth.

Income and wealth aren't entirely independent. Income is a potential source of wealth, but wealth can also generate income.
Income is how much you earn.
Wealth is what you accumulate and retain.

If your only source of income is your labor, you'll always have to work for your money. As you accumulate wealth, you can become less dependent on employment income. Your accumulated assets can generate income, appreciate in value, or both.

Instead of working for every dollar, you have something that produces money for you.
The more productive assets you accumulate, the more your money can work on your behalf.
It's like owning a legal money-printing machine.
Who wouldn't want a legal money-printing machine in their basement or garage?

Of course, there is no such thing as a legal money-printing machine.
But productive assets can serve a similar purpose.

Think of the financial concepts and instruments you use to generate and manage wealth as your financial toolkit.

Your financial toolkit consists of several basic components:
* [Asset Generation](#asset-generation)
* [Asset Instruments](#asset-instruments)
* [Asset Management](#asset-management)

## Asset Generation
Wealth doesn't appear on its own. You need a way to acquire it and make it grow.

### Parents
Our parents are financially responsible for us while we're children.

Some parents extend financial support to their adult children as well by paying for their college education, contributing toward a first home and beyond.

Some children will inherit substantial wealth from their parents.
Trillions of US dollars will be transferred from the older generations to younger generations through the [Great Wealth Transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wealth_Transfer). 
However, inheritance is not evenly distributed from one generation to the next, and you cannot determine when you receive it.

Your best bet for parental support and a significant inheritance is to choose your parents wisely.

Regardless of parental support, the next two asset generators are available to most people.

### You
For most people, income earned over a lifetime is the primary source of wealth generation.
 
You generate income, but you also need food, shelter, clothing, transportation, insurance, etc.
You earn a lot, but you spend a lot, too.
You may be a great asset, but you are also a high-maintenance expense.

How much of your income have you put toward building wealth?
How much of your income have you spent?

To build wealth from your income, your income must exceed your expenses.
Even those with high incomes may fail to generate wealth if their expenses are too large.
Inflation and lifestyle creep can erode what once felt like a monumental salary.

### Time
The earlier you start saving and investing, the easier it will be to reach your financial goals.
This isn’t just because you have more time to put more money away.
It’s because the money you invest has more time to grow through [compound interest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_interest).
 
Compounding creates a snowball effect.
When you’re rolling a snowball to make a snowman, the snowball will reach a sufficient size so that it will start to pick up snow from the ground just by rolling it.
 
With investments, earnings start to produce their own earnings, which produce their own earnings, etc.
Albert Einstein is often credited with calling compound interest the eighth wonder of the world, although there's little evidence he actually said it.

#### A Comparison Example

**NOTE:** This example assumes a constant 10% annual return, which is unrealistic. It's illustrating the power of compounding rather than representing actual investment performance. The example excludes taxes, fees, and withdrawals.

Twins Anna and Billy start their careers when they graduate from college at age 21. 

Anna decides to invest $1,000 annually at the beginning of each year toward retirement in her company’s retirement plan.
It’s tight at first, but it gets a little easier with several raises.
Then Anna gets married and starts a family and decides to become a stay-at-home mom at age 28. 
Anna does not make additional contributions to her retirement account after she stops working.

Her brother, Billy, takes a different approach. He waits to start investing.
He has always intended to start saving for retirement, but he never got around to filling out the paperwork with Human Resources.
When he sees his sister start a family, he decides to get serious about his future as well, and he visits his Human Resources representative to start.
He invests $1,000 annually at the beginning of each year, also starting at age 29 and continuing until he retires at age 65.

Both twins earn an average of 10% on their investments annually.
 
At age 65, Billy has invested a grand total of $37,000; however, his account contains around $363,000. He has around 10 dollars for every dollar he has invested during his career!

At age 65, Anna has only invested $8,000 during her short career, which is less than a quarter of what her brother has invested. However, Anna’s account contains $427,800! She has over 50 dollars for every dollar she has invested.
Those eight early years made all the difference, thanks to compound interest.

When Anna stops investing, her eight $1,000 contributions have already grown to almost $12,600 due to compound interest.
Billy must supply $1,000 of new money from his income.
Anna's growth continues from her head start investment.
Her retirement fund continues to grow, even as Billy contributes another $1,000 each year.
The earnings on Anna's larger balance exceed Billy's annual contribution.
The gap between their balances will widen.
Anna's early start gives her an advantage that Billy can overcome only by dedicating more of his income toward retirement.

## Asset Instruments
Assets can take several forms.

### Cash and Cash Equivalents
Cash is king.
Cash provides liquidity, convenience, and certainty about its nominal value.
It's useful for paying expenses and handling emergencies.
But it generally isn't a great long-term wealth-building instrument because inflation erodes its purchasing power.

When discussing personal finances, we often use the term cash to describe money that is readily available for spending. This includes physical currency as well as electronic balances in financial accounts.

Some financial products aren't strictly cash, but they are so similar in their liquidity and stability that they're considered cash equivalents.

Strictly speaking, however, a checking or savings account isn't cash.
It's a financial product that holds your money and provides convenient access to it.
Because those funds are readily available, these accounts are often treated as cash equivalents.

In the United States, the [Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation](https://www.fdic.gov/resources/deposit-insurance) (FDIC) insures eligible deposits at insured banks up to $250,000 per depositor, per insured bank, per ownership category.
There are additional rules and restrictions, but the coverage depends on the depositor's account ownership and the amount deposited.
This insurance protects most deposits should an insured bank fail.

Checking accounts, savings accounts, money market deposit accounts, and eligible CDs at FDIC-insured banks generally qualify for coverage, subject to the applicable limits.

Money market mutual funds and Treasury bills are not bank deposits and are not covered by FDIC insurance.

#### Cash
Cash is a useful tool, especially for recurring expenses, but cash is a limited tool.
Cash doesn't generally generate much of a return.
Even when it earns interest, inflation can erode its purchasing power over time.
 
Cash does have its uses.
It is useful for its liquidity and also for its stability when the stock market takes a downturn.
For those who tend to slip back into debt, cash may be a primary tool for managing household finances to prevent sinking further into debt.

#### Checking Account
Checking accounts are used to manage day-to-day financial transactions, such as paying bills and making purchases.

#### Savings Account
Savings accounts are places to hold money while earning modest interest.

Money can often be easily transferred between checking and savings accounts.

##### High-Yield Savings Account

A high-yield savings account is like a traditional savings account, but it generally pays a higher interest rate.

These accounts are often offered by online banks, which may have lower operating costs than traditional banks.

Like traditional savings accounts, eligible deposits at FDIC-insured banks are protected by FDIC insurance, subject to applicable limits.

#### Money Market Account
A money market deposit account is like a savings/checking account on steroids.
Money market accounts combine some of the features of checking and savings accounts.
They are often used to hold larger balances and often pay more interest than traditional savings accounts.
They may also impose restrictions on withdrawals, transfers, or minimum balances.

#### Money Market Mutual Fund
A money market mutual fund is an investment fund that provides a convenient place to hold cash while earning a return.

Money market mutual funds pool money from many investors and use it to purchase short-term, relatively low-risk investments, such as Treasury bills, certificates of deposit, and commercial paper.

They are often used to hold larger amounts of cash and generally provide convenient access to your money.
They generally offer competitive interest rates.

Unlike a bank money market account, a money market mutual fund is an investment, not a bank deposit.
It is not insured by the FDIC, and its value and returns are not guaranteed.
Although these funds are designed to maintain a stable share price, they can lose value.

#### Certificate of Deposit
[Certificates of Deposit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Certificate_of_deposit) (CD) are generally very safe when held at an FDIC-insured bank and within applicable insurance limits. Their interest rates are usually fixed, but the money is generally expected to remain on deposit until maturity. There may be penalties for early withdrawal.

#### Treasury Bills
[U.S. Treasury bills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Treasury_security) (T-bills) are short-term loans to the federal government, generally maturing in four to 52 weeks.
T-bills are generally purchased for less than their face value.
At maturity, the government pays the face value, and the difference represents your return.
They are exempt from state and local income taxes on their interest.

They're generally considered very safe because they're backed by the U.S. government. They can also be sold before maturity, although the price received may differ from the purchase price.

They're a useful example of an instrument that isn't technically cash but can serve a cash-like role.

### Loans
A loan is an arrangement in which one party lends an asset, usually money, to another party with the expectation that it will be repaid, usually with interest.

Principal is the amount borrowed.
Interest is the cost of borrowing money, generally expressed as a percentage of the outstanding principal.

A [bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_%28finance%29) is a formal debt security that specifies the terms of a loan.
In buying a bond, you're lending money to a person or institution that promises to repay the principal according to specified terms, often with interest.

Unlike a typical personal loan or mortgage, a bond can generally be bought and sold by investors in financial markets. This allows the original lender to sell the debt to someone else rather than waiting for the borrower to repay it.
The maturity date is when the issuer is due to repay the bond's face value (principal).
You may be able to sell a bond before maturity, but its market value may be higher or lower than what you paid.
The money you lend may be at risk, depending upon the type of bond.
 
Common types of bonds are government bonds and corporate bonds.
Governments and corporations issue bonds to borrow money for projects, operations, or other financial obligations.
A government may borrow money by issuing a bond to build a new bridge.
A business may borrow money to purchase another company.

Government and corporate borrowing is not much different than taking out student loans to pay for college or taking on a mortgage to purchase a home.
Borrowing money finances a purchase that might not be possible by any other means.

Loans can be secured or unsecured:
* Secured loans are backed by collateral, such as a home or automobile. If the borrower defaults, the lender may have the right to repossess the collateral or foreclose on the property.
* Unsecured loans aren't backed by specific collateral. Credit cards and many personal loans are examples.

The borrower intends to repay the loan, but there is no guarantee.
Sometimes borrowers default, meaning they fail to meet their repayment obligations.
The lender may be able to recover some of the money through collateral or legal proceedings but could lose some or all of the investment.

Not all borrowers are equally creditworthy.
The interest rate depends partly on how likely the lender thinks the borrower is to repay the loan. 
Lenders generally demand higher interest rates from borrowers they consider more likely to default, compensating them for taking on greater risk.

Organizations such as [S&P Global Ratings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%26P_Global_Ratings), [Moody's Ratings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moody%27s_Ratings), and [Fitch Ratings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitch_Ratings) assign credit ratings to governments, corporations, and their debt obligations.
These ratings represent opinions on creditworthiness rather than guarantees, helping investors evaluate the likelihood that a borrower will meet its debt obligations.
These ratings use letter grades, often with additional distinctions within each grade, to indicate the issuer's creditworthiness.

### Ownership
One way to generate wealth is to own things that have value.

Those things may appreciate over time, generate income, or both. You can own an individual company, a collection of investments, or other assets.

#### Stock
[Stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock) is ownership. When you buy a stock, you own a piece of that company.

While issuing bonds is a way for an institution to borrow money, issuing stock is a way to raise money by selling equity. Nascent companies often obtain initial funding from founders and early investors. This seed money helps cover early operations, such as hiring employees, renting space, and building products.

As a company matures, it aims to become profitable by generating more revenue than it spends. Early investors accumulate wealth as the company’s value grows, but that wealth remains tied up in private ownership. To unlock liquidity, raise capital for expansion, or allow early owners to sell shares, a company may go public through an [Initial Public Offering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_public_offering) (IPO), listing its shares on a public stock exchange. Becoming public isn't mandatory; many successful businesses remain private to keep their financial disclosures confidential.

When a company is profitable, its board of directors has choices on how to deploy those profits. For example, if a company sells a [widget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widget_(economics)) for $15 and incurs $10 in total costs, it nets $5 in profit before taxes. It can reinvest those earnings into research and development, buy back shares on the open market, or distribute a portion directly to shareholders as dividends. Unlike interest on a bond, which is a strict contractual obligation, dividends are discretionary distributions authorized by the board.

Investors evaluate stocks across several dimensions:
* **Growth vs. Value:** Growth stocks belong to companies expected to expand revenues quickly, while value stocks appear bargain-priced relative to current fundamentals and often pay steady dividends. (These are investment styles rather than strict categories; companies can exhibit traits of both.)
* **Market Capitalization:** Stocks are categorized by total market value (share price multiplied by total outstanding shares) into small-cap, mid-cap, and large-cap, across domestic and international markets.

Owning common stock often grants voting rights, typically one vote per share, for electing the [board of directors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Board_of_directors) or voting on major corporate decisions. Shareholders can also attend annual meetings.

Many years ago at an AT&T shareholder meeting, a retired investor addressed [CEO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chief_executive_officer) [Bob Allen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_E._Allen_(telecommunications_executive)): "I'm a millionaire due to your leadership." Allen smiled, until the gentleman added, "I used to be a multi-millionaire."

Stock ownership offers two avenues for returns: capital appreciation and dividends. However, neither is guaranteed, and individual stock prices can fluctuate significantly based on company performance and market conditions.

#### Liquidity and the Secondary Market
A bond has a maturity date years in the future, and a stock may appreciate significantly, but neither is legal tender. You can't walk into a store and use shares of stock or a corporate bond to buy a new household appliance.

Accumulating assets builds your net worth, but a portfolio can easily leave you asset-rich and cash-poor if your wealth is tied up in non-cash holdings. What happens when you need liquidity to make a major purchase?

Fortunately, secondary markets exist so you can sell assets to other willing buyers. Publicly traded stocks can be bought and sold in minutes on exchanges like the [New York Stock Exchange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Stock_Exchange) (NYSE) or the [NASDAQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasdaq). Other assets have their own markets, such as bonds, commodities, and real estate each operate on different timelines, where liquidating a stock takes minutes, while selling real estate may require weeks or months.

You are never locked into holding a stock or a bond indefinitely. You can offload your position before maturity or before a company exits, but the price you receive depends entirely on what a buyer is willing to pay at that moment, which may be higher or lower than your original purchase price.

#### Mutual Funds
A [mutual fund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutual_fund) is an investment vehicle that pools money from many investors to purchase a collection of assets. Think of a mutual fund as a bucket that holds stocks, bonds, or real estate. Rather than buying a single company's stock or bond, you buy shares of the fund itself, giving you proportional ownership of every asset in the portfolio.

A primary advantage of mutual funds is diversification. Spreading capital across dozens or hundreds of holdings reduces the impact of any single company failing, though it does not eliminate broader market risk.

A mutual fund's per-share value is called its [Net Asset Value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_asset_value) (NAV). NAV is calculated at the close of each trading day by subtracting the fund's liabilities from the total market value of its holdings and dividing by total outstanding shares.

Funds generate returns through price appreciation and distributions (dividends, interest, and realized capital gains). Distributions can be taken as cash or automatically reinvested into additional shares. Importantly, reinvested distributions may still trigger tax obligations in taxable accounts.

##### Management Styles and Fees
Offered by financial firms like [Vanguard](https://investor.vanguard.com/) and [Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/), mutual funds generally fall into several management styles:
* **Actively Managed Funds:** Portfolio managers select specific assets attempting to beat market benchmarks. Because they require research teams and active trading, they charge higher operational costs, known as an expense ratio. Some funds also charge sales commissions, or loads.
* **Passively Managed Index Funds:** These funds simply replicate published benchmarks like the [S&P 500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%26P_500) or [Dow Jones Industrial Average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dow_Jones_Industrial_Average). While index funds may seem dull, their significantly lower expense ratios and broad exposure allow them to outperform most actively managed funds over the long term.
* **Target Retirement Funds:** These are retirement funds targeted to a specific retirement date. For example, someone born in 2000 might invest in a 2060 or 2065 target retirement fund designed for retirement when the investor is 60 or 65 in each of those years respectively. I'll provide more details in the next blog (TBD).

Funds tailor their strategies to specific investment objectives, such as focusing on growth stocks, value stocks, specific industry sectors, or bond markets. Some focus on _socially responsible_ or _ESG_ (_environmental, social, and governance_) criteria, selecting assets based on sustainability, labor standards, or corporate ethics while avoiding industries like defense or tobacco.

#### ETFs
[Exchange Traded Funds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange-traded_fund) (ETFs) are investment vehicles that pool money from many investors to purchase a collection of assets. Like mutual funds, they allow you to invest in a portfolio of stocks, bonds, or other investments by purchasing shares of the fund.

ETFs are similar to mutual funds in many ways. They provide access to a collection of investments, can offer diversification, and generally have a fund manager responsible for managing the fund's assets.

However, there is an important difference in how you buy and sell them.

Unlike mutual funds, which generally transact once each business day at their Net Asset Value (NAV), ETFs trade on stock exchanges throughout the trading day, just like individual stocks. You can buy or sell ETF shares whenever the market is open, and their prices fluctuate throughout the day based on supply and demand.

An ETF's market price is generally close to its NAV, but it can trade at a slight premium or discount to the value of its underlying assets.

ETFs can be actively managed or passively managed. Many ETFs are index funds that track published indexes, such as the [S&P 500](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S%26P_500) or [NASDAQ-100](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nasdaq-100). Others focus on particular sectors, investment categories, or objectives. Actively managed ETFs have managers who select investments attempting to outperform the market.

Some well-known ETFs include [SPDR S&P 500 ETF Trust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SPDR_S%26P_500_ETF_Trust), commonly known by its ticker symbol **SPY**, and [Invesco QQQ ETF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invesco_QQQ), commonly known as **QQQ**. SPY tracks the S&P 500, while QQQ tracks the NASDAQ-100.

Like mutual funds, ETFs have expense ratios that represent the annual cost of operating the fund as a percentage of its average net assets. Index ETFs typically have relatively low expense ratios, although costs vary by fund.

ETFs may also distribute income earned from dividends and interest, as well as realized capital gains. These distributions can be paid in cash or reinvested in additional shares and may have tax implications.

ETFs can have some tax advantages over comparable mutual funds. Their structure allows many ETFs to minimize capital gains distributions by transferring securities rather than selling them when investors redeem shares. However, this doesn't mean ETF investors are exempt from taxes. Distributions and gains from selling ETF shares may still be taxable.

ETFs are often a convenient and relatively inexpensive way to invest in a diversified portfolio. However, not every ETF is broadly diversified. Some concentrate on particular industries, market segments, or investment strategies. As with mutual funds, it's important to understand what a fund owns and what it is designed to accomplish.

#### Mutual Funds vs. ETFs
Mutual funds and ETFs offer many of the same investment opportunities. Both allow investors to pool their money to purchase a diversified collection of assets. Both can be actively managed or passively track an index.

So why choose one over the other?

**Reasons to choose a mutual fund:**
* You prefer to make investments at the end of the trading day, without worrying about intraday price fluctuations.
* You want to invest a specific dollar amount, including fractional shares.
* You want to automate regular investments, such as monthly contributions.
* Your employer-sponsored retirement plan offers mutual funds as its investment options.

**Reasons to choose an ETF:**
* You want to buy and sell shares throughout the trading day.
* You prefer to see market prices and control the price at which you place an order.
* You want access to potentially lower-cost investment options.
* You want the potential tax advantages that ETFs may offer over comparable mutual funds.

Neither investment vehicle is inherently better. The right choice depends on your investment objectives, costs, account type, and personal preferences.

#### Home Ownership
Homeownership is often considered a central pillar of the American Dream, but it raises a fundamental financial question: Is a house an asset or a liability?

Strictly speaking, the physical property is an asset, while the mortgage used to buy it is a liability. Owning a home comes with significant ongoing operational expenses. Unlike renting, where the monthly rent is the maximum you will pay for housing that month, a mortgage payment is the minimum. Homeowners are responsible for maintenance, utilities, home insurance, and property taxes, which can be substantial.

Mortgages are also expensive long-term financial commitments. Over a 30-year loan, total principal and interest payments can easily amount to two or more times the original purchase price, depending on prevailing interest rates. (In the U.S., mortgage interest may be tax-deductible if you itemize deductions, subject to IRS limits.)

Despite these costs, homeownership can be an effective vehicle for wealth accumulation. Real estate often appreciates over time, forced savings occur as you pay down mortgage principal, and once the debt is paid off, a major recurring expense disappears from your budget. Under the right financial conditions, a home can serve as a foundation for intergenerational wealth.

#### Retirement Plans
In the United States, specialized tax-advantaged accounts, such as [401(k)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/401(k)) plans and [Individual Retirement Accounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Individual_retirement_account) (IRAs), are designed to encourage long-term saving.

It's helpful to think of these plans not as investments themselves, but as tax-sheltered _wrappers_ or containers that hold investments like stocks, bonds, mutual funds, and ETFs.

Because these account rules and tax incentives are specific to the United States, they deserve dedicated attention and will be the primary focus in a subsequent entry (TBD) in this series.

# Asset Management
Managing your wealth isn't just about picking individual assets.
It's about how you assemble, monitor, and adjust them over time.

## Asset Classes
There is no perfect asset.
Ideally, an asset would:
* Never lose value
* Have a great rate of return
* Pay interest or a dividend
* Be effortless converted into cash

It does not exist.

I listed several set of asset above, including: [Cash and Cash Equivalents](#cash-and-cash-equivalents), [Bonds](#loans) and [Stocks](#stock), [Mutual Funds](#mutual-funds), and [ETFs](#etfs).
I'm going to refer to these as _asset classes_.
That is, it's a group of different specific assets that have shared traits.
For example, while stocks have similar traits, there are thousands of companies that issue stock.
These are broad asset classes as well.
There can be significant distinctions within asset classes as well.
For example, equities span high-growth tech startups and dividend-paying utilities, while bonds range from ultra-safe U.S. Treasury bills to high-yield corporate debt.
A tech stock will have different traits than a restaurant stock.

Each of these asset classes has traits associated with an ideal asset, but none of them have the entire package.
Each asset class has its own advantages and disadvantages.
Each asset class has its niche when 

Cash, and cash equivalents, are very safe, but since they don't grow over time, their purchasing power erodes with inflation.

Bonds tend to be relatively safe as well. They generate stable interest and dividends and tend to keep up with inflation, but there's not much growth.

Stocks tend to have the greatest risks and reward along with volatility. They produce your greatest gains and losses, and fortunes can swing between the two quickly.

## Asset Allocation
A well funded retirement is a financial goal.
While this blog series has focused upon retirement, retirement is generally not the only financial goal for a person or household.
Other common financial goals include:
* Purchase of a car
* Down payment for a house
* College fund for children
* Paying off student loans

Different financial goals have different amounts required and different time horizons.
Some goals are near term, such as a car purchase and paying off student loans.
Some goals are further away, such as a college fund for a child or retirement, but even far away goals creep up upon us.

Contributing toward all goals at the same time may not be an option either.
A household with a $1,000 per month financial margin has a better chance of funding its goals than a household with a $100 per month financial margin.
Goal funding and/or financial margins may require adjustment.

Different goals have different risk/reward sensitivity based upon their time horizon.
Asset class risk/reward should be aligned to the risk/reward sensitivity of the goals goals they are funding.
In general, the further away the goal is, the greater the risk/reward it can withstand, since there's time to recover from any downward volatility.

Determining which assets classes based upon their risk/reward profile are best aligned with financial goals especially based upon risk/reward sensitivity of their timeline is referred to as _Asset Allocation_.

Let's consider a family with 35-year old parents with a ten year old child with the following goals:
* Car fund to replace one of the current cars in two years. This fund should be in CDs and bonds, since a new car purchase is right around the corner.
* College fund for their child. This fund should be a mixture of stocks and bonds. The child won't start college for another eight years. The stocks will provide some growth for the interim, and the bonds will provide some stability should the stock market run into a recession.
* Retirement fund for the parents. This fund should be almost entirely in stocks, since retirement is still 30 years away. However, they will want to consider adding bonds in the next five to ten years.

Asset allocation changes as time advances.
The 35-year old couple can absorb the volatility of stocks 30 years from retirement, but when they reach retirement at age 65 a portion of their retirement fund should have cash and bonds.
And it should still retain some stock too.
The 65-year old couple could easily have a 30-year retirement.
They would still need some stocks for growth along with their cash and bonds.

Goal targeted funds often contain different asset classes, as described in the previous blog (TBD)
Different asset classes respond differently to different market conditions.
Therefore rebalancing between asset classes may be warrented.

For example, let's assume that our 65-year old couple wants their retirement asset allocation to be 50% stocks and 50% bonds.
One year the stock market does great, but the bond market does not.
Their allocation changes to 60% stocks and 40% bonds, not because of anything they did, but because of market conditions.
Therefore, they may rebalance their portfolio by purchasing more bonds with future contributions rather than stock to bring their asset allocation closer a 50/50 balance.
They may even sell some stock and purchase bonds to bring the ratio closer to 50/50.

## Asset Diversification
In addition to the correct asset allocation, the assets should have some diversity.
That is, don't put all your eggs in one basket.

While the 30-year old couple's retirement fund should mostly be invested in stocks, it should not be invested in one stock.
Everyone dreams of buying Amazon, Google, Nvidia or Apple before they became big.
But for every company that would have made you a fortune, there was an equally compelling company that would have cost you a fortune, such as [Enron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enron).

One of the best ways to get diversification is through [Mutual Funds](#mutual-funds) and [ETFs](#exchange-traded-funds-or-etfs).
Diversification is built into their very nature.

Some financial companies offer target retirement based mutual funds as mentioned in the previous blog.
They are mutual funds with a specific target date in mind.
Targeted based mutual funds will readjust and rebalance the asset allocation among its asset classes automatically.
As the target date approaches, the mutual fund will reallocate asset class ratios adjusting for volatility over the years as well as rebalancing to ensure that the ratios are maintained.

Targeted funds can be good for retirement or a child's college fund.
These goals have a time horizon that's often decades long when first opened.
Their asset allocation profile will change as time advances to the start date.
Rebalancing and reallocation happens automatically for the investor through the years.
